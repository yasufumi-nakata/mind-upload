The three routes can advance in parallel. These are this site’s research proposals. Make the first deliverable a reproduced result, one changed condition and a comparison with the original.

Wiring and data analysis

Run a public fly model and measure how added wiring errors alter predictions. Deliver a comparison and reproducible code that inform acquisition accuracy.[1][2]

Make a small result useful to the next step: record data and model versions, changed conditions, separation of training and evaluation data, results and failure conditions. A result that others can reproduce and use as the basis for another experiment extends the research path.

Reproduce public fly models and test structural errors

A first step: reproduce one Shiu prediction from sugar sensory input to MN9, then introduce one kind of connectivity error. The Lappalainen visual model is a later candidate for testing generality across representations and tasks. The following is this site’s proposed experiment.

Check manipulation scope and existing controls

Over-segmentation and false merges require additional tests defining how image-segmentation errors change connectivity. Even in Shiu, specify reassignment of cell inputs, outputs and states. Introducing neuron-specific errors in Lappalainen requires a derived model that relaxes connection sharing across columns. Report this separately from C1 in the original model.

Shiu et al. reported MN9 firing in only 1 of 100 shuffled trials under 100 Hz sugar sensory input, versus all trials with correct wiring, while preserving the global weight distribution. Reproduce that implementation first. Additional degree-preserving or type-preserving controls can separate properties disrupted together in the original shuffle.[1]

This table screens candidates; it does not establish that comparable C4 experiments are already feasible. Publish correspondence, inputs/outputs, sex, regions, cell counts, reconstruction completeness, transmitter estimates, data versions and mapping uncertainty. Prespecify treatment of inputs outside shared coverage. Hemispheric differences do not replace animal-level variation. Without functional data from the same animal, report variation in connectome-model predictions.[14]

Advance by: publishing sensitivity curves for each error unit, task and fixed/refit condition. In a different model, first test whether sensitivity trends generalize. Claiming reproduction of the same numerical tolerance requires a prespecified common error scale, such as the fraction of changed synapses, plus comparable metrics and tolerances.

Separate preparation, completion and success

  1. Prepare: record data/code versions, reuse terms, circuit scope, the target figure or table and inputs. Narrow the experiment if required data are unavailable.
  2. Pilot: measure memory, runtime and storage; budget all conditions and multiple seeds. Do not infer runtime from paper scale alone.

  3. Prespecify: first choose metrics, tolerances and meaningful minimum effects. Then record comparison conditions.

    • Error units and ranges
    • Fixed-parameter or refit testing, and adjustable quantities
    • Training/evaluation separation
    • Seeds, compute budgets and model-selection rules
  4. Completion: publish C0 and feasible C1–C4 comparisons, identifying exclusions and unperformed tests. A first experiment may use only fixed parameters and one error type.
  5. Success: verify C0 reproduction, report differences from suitable controls, and identify prediction-preserving errors under the specified fixed/refit regime. In another model, first test generality of sensitivity trends. Prespecify a common scale before comparing identical numerical tolerances. Without a biological tolerance, report a provisional sensitivity analysis.

If C0 cannot be reproduced, investigate versions and preprocessing before reporting a reproducibility problem. If scores never change, examine metric sensitivity; if tiny errors collapse performance, investigate model instability first.

Next research tasks and available infrastructure

After bounding required accuracy, follow-up projects can examine measurement choice in known artificial circuits, held-out activity prediction in ZAPBench, and paired structure–function measurements. Choose a platform for the question.[4][15][16]

  • NEURON: infrastructure for cellular and circuit simulation.
  • Brain Modeling ToolKit (BMTK): infrastructure for constructing models at different levels of detail.
  • SpiNNaker: an example architecture for communicating large numbers of spikes.

Distinguish what the papers evaluated from the capabilities of the software version used in a new experiment.[5][6][17]

Research tasks by area of expertise

Experimental neuroscience: structure–activity registration, interventions, longitudinal recordings. Microscopy and image analysis: throughput, deformation correction, segmentation, proofreading. Mathematics and machine learning: inverse problems, identifiability, uncertainty, generalization. Computing: memory, distributed execution, reproducibility. Literature curation: maintain links among DOI, version, corrections, data, and code.

Turn the ambition of running a brain into small, falsifiable deliverables that resolve a necessary part of the problem.

Six experiments built from the literature

These are this guide's proposed experiments, not completed results. They connect evidence using existing R01–R12 IDs. Meet data-use conditions and necessary review/consent requirements before starting.

  1. R01–R02: connect proofreading errors to functional errors. Freeze a dataset such as FlyWire, then inject false merges, splits and missing synapses separately. Compare degree-preserving controls and equal-budget fits with/without refitting. Deliver error-versus-held-out-performance curves. If tolerances fail, prioritize proofreading or weight measurements over more imaging.[8][18][19]
  2. R03–R04: separate speed from missed observations. Define distinct voltage and calcium observation models. Vary downsampling, noise and missing cells on synthetic data with known spikes. Separate animals and trials when scoring prediction and detection; target additional measurements to failure regions.[20][15]
  3. R05–R06: compare candidate dynamics and learning rules. Match budgets for connectivity-only, activity-only, combined and additional-state models. If unseen interventions or reversal learning cannot distinguish them, select interventions where predictions diverge. Deliver remaining candidates and identifying conditions, not just fitted values.[2][21][22]
  4. R07–R08: measure structural and functional preservation together. Compare fresh, agent-only and preserved tissue; retain failures in denominators. Score structure, metabolism, electrophysiology and plasticity separately by cell type and region. Design an independent retained-memory test before treating plasticity as memory recovery.[23][24][25]
  5. R09–R10: test dependence on the body model. Compare open and closed loops for one neural model while varying latency, terrain and sensory noise. Label biological connectivity, trained controllers and engineered components separately. Establish reproducibility in another implementation before extending to long-term learning.[26][7][27]
  6. R11–R12: independently recompute individual correspondence. Freeze structure versions, common clocks, cell matches, exclusions, seeds and execution environments. Compare own-individual, other-individual and average models on memory tests whose answers never enter fitting. Test confidence intervals against prespecified equivalence bounds; insufficient discrimination calls for more data, not an equivalence claim.[28][29][30]

First deliverable: one small benchmark containing a fixed dataset version, minimal controls, held-out split, rerun instructions and failure criteria. Expand to another individual or circuit after success. Publication counts do not yield deadlines or success probabilities.
