History and current progress: the gates to 2050

See what works, what remains, and the evidence needed to advance. A conditional research plan targeting technical completion in 2050.

The current position at a glance

6research domains

12major open tasks

5review gates to 2050

Current position: small-animal wiring maps, local mammalian structure–activity pairing, and predictions of limited functions. The next step is to connect these in the same individual and test reproduction that retains previously learned information.[1][2][3][4]

The central gap: what information, measured at what precision, is sufficient to recover an individual’s functions and memories? The reviewed literature does not demonstrate human whole-brain reproduction retaining individual memories.[5][6]

The six domains and 12 tasks are this site’s organisation of the problem. Task counts do not measure research effort or probability of success. Organisation updated: 2026-11-06. Individual source access dates are recorded in the library.

Six domains and 12 remaining tasks

Each card shows demonstrated capabilities and the next barrier. Expand it for the major tasks that still need evidence. Work proceeds in parallel, with measurements and models informing each other.

01

Map the wiring

Small animals / local tissue

Now FlyWire reconstructed 139,255 neurons in an adult female brain. BANC and MaleCNS are separate brain-and-cord resources. The mammalian MICrONS dataset covers part of visual cortex.[1][7][8][2]

Two major remaining tasks
  1. R01 Choose resolution and tolerable missing/false connections using functional tests.
  2. R02 Measure whole-brain throughput and cost, including cross-section tracing and automated correction.

Next barrier Whole mammalian brains; control wiring errors

02

Read activity and state

Large recordings; partial pairing

Now ZAPBench uses calcium activity from over 70,000 neurons in a larval zebrafish. MICrONS manually matched 15,439 cells between functional recordings and EM. Recording activity does not read every relevant state variable.[9][2]

Two major remaining tasks
  1. R03 Separate temporal resolution and measurement noise from unobserved states. Evaluate glial activity, peptide release and receptor expression as candidates for additional measurement.
  2. R04 Pair structure, activity and necessary molecular data in one individual; reserve evaluation data. Test which functions tolerate omitting extrasynaptic signals.

Next barrier Pair state and molecular data in one individual

03

Reconstruct function

Predictions tested on limited tasks

Now Shiu et al. tested fly sensorimotor functions; Lappalainen et al. compared a connectome-constrained visual model with experimental data. Neither establishes recovery of all functions or the original individual’s learning history.[3][4]

Two major remaining tasks
  1. R05 Infer transmission properties and necessary states from measurements; test unseen stimuli and interventions.
  2. R06 Include learning rules and test retention, forgetting and relearning over time.

Next barrier Unseen interventions; learning and plasticity

04

Preserve the needed information

Ultrastructure preserved

Now ASC demonstrated preserved ultrastructure in rabbit and pig brain tissue, not restored function or memory. Local engram-structure studies are also distinct from methods for reading arbitrary memories.[10][11]

Two major remaining tasks
  1. R07 Measure structural and molecular losses during preservation, fixation and imaging. For glia and extrasynaptic signalling, distinguish retained morphology from retained functionally necessary states.
  2. R08 Test whether preserved information supports an independent memory task in a previously trained individual.

Next barrier Show that memory-relevant information survives

05

Compute and connect to a body

Platforms and limited models

Now NEURON, BMTK and SpiNNaker support neural-model computation. BAAIWorm links neural, body and environmental models but covers limited neurons and behaviours. Processor arithmetic throughput alone does not establish WBE feasibility.[12][13][14][15][5]

Two major remaining tasks
  1. R09 Measure memory, communication, energy and speed relative to real time at the required model detail.
  2. R10 Test long runs and state save/resume with sensory feedback and learning.

Next barrier Whole-brain cost, latency and long-run stability

06

Validate the original individual

Frameworks; integrated goal unmet

Now The Brain Emulation Challenge compares reconstruction methods on synthetic circuits with known ground truth. Fidelity research proposes purpose-dependent functional tests. The reviewed literature does not demonstrate whole-brain reproduction retaining a human individual’s memories.[16][5][6]

Two major remaining tasks
  1. R11 Use average and other-individual controls; evaluate held-out individual responses and memories under blinded assessment.
  2. R12 Reproduce results with independent teams and additional individuals; report failure boundaries.

Next barrier Validate the full chain, including individual memory

Five stages for testing a 2050 scenario

These are proposed target windows set by this site. They are not completion forecasts derived from the literature or Carboncopies Foundation’s official schedule. If an earlier gate remains unmet, later dates change. Windows mark reviews of achieved capabilities, not the first year work begins.

Each year is an editorial review window spaced five years apart to compare progress. These are not completion dates derived from measured throughput or success probabilities; unmet conditions move later windows back.

  1. 2030 review

    Establish measurements and error limits

    What must be measured, and how well?

    Gate and fallback

    2026–2030 Advance M1–M2 with synthetic circuits and existing fly models. Produce paired structure/activity data, reproduction code and links between reconstruction error and function. Measure baseline acquisition/correction throughput, costs and computing resources.

    Pass: Validate held-out circuits, stimuli and interventions, and reproducibly identify errors that break predictions. Report acquisition years V/(12qn) from tissue volume V, effective monthly throughput q per instrument and instrument count n; measure preparation, correction and independent-validation time and cost separately.

    If unmet: If reproduction fails, correct data versions, measurements or models before approving scale-up.

  2. 2035 review

    Demonstrate individual and memory fidelity

    Test the core idea in small systems

    Gate and fallback

    2031–2035 For M3–M4, reconstruct responses from structure and state in trained small animals or local mammalian circuits. Compare individuals with different learning histories.

    Pass: Without training on memory-test answers, the original-individual model meets prespecified superiority criteria against average/other-individual controls, with independent replication. Test sensory feedback for selected behaviours; report individuals, sessions, δ, ε and interval estimates. Update measured acquisition/correction throughput and cost.

    If unmet: Add molecular or functional data if structure is insufficient. Reconsider the acquisition route for humans if information sufficiency remains unsupported.

  3. 2040 review

    Integrate and scale in mammals

    Bridge partial success to whole brains

    Gate and fallback

    2036–2040 Connect imaging, reconstruction, modelling and body interaction while expanding to larger mammalian circuits, targeting whole-brain coverage and long-term learning tests.

    Pass: Retain individual-difference and learning tests at scale; measure missing coverage, total time, cost, memory and real-time ratio. Automated tracing/correction must meet whole-brain error tolerances; include person-hours and cost wherever manual correction remains.

    If unmet: Revise acquisition, model detail or parallelisation if fidelity or cost misses the criteria. A completed wiring map alone does not pass this gate.

  4. 2045 review

    Establish conditions for human application

    Test species differences and whole-brain acquisition

    Gate and fallback

    2041–2045 Test animal-derived assumptions in human cells and local circuits. Establish a design linking prior recordings to acquired data from the same individual, plus whole-brain acquisition and modelling workflows.

    Pass: Preregister prior-recording modalities, duration, repeats, spatial coverage and held-out evaluation data for each target function. Record acquisition delay, terminal condition, fixation and molecular/structural losses. Include review, consent, sample-provision arrangements and their lead times. Passing requires adequate comparison power and end-to-end resources.

    If unmet: Reset the 2050 integration target if necessary human states cannot be measured or preserved, or valid comparison with the original individual is unavailable.

  5. 2050 review

    Independently test human whole-brain reproduction

    An integrated prototype that passes the gates

    Gate and fallback

    2046–2050 Only after the preceding gates are met, build a whole-brain model from a particular person’s measurements and evaluate activity, previously learned information, learning capacity and body interaction together.

    Pass: Use held-out prior recordings and assess how acquisition conditions affect function. Test individual correspondence, advantages over average/other-individual controls, independent replication and operational stability at prespecified functions/timescales. Fitting imaging alone into five years would require about 6.8× ExLSFM or 333.4× EM aggregate throughput relative to rounded Table 3 estimates. Preparation, reconstruction, correction and functional validation need separate time allowances.[17]

    If unmet: Report partial success at its demonstrated functional and anatomical scope; do not declare the whole-brain target met by skipping remaining conditions.

The 2030 and 2035 stages correspond to M1–M4. Later stages propose scaling those tests and establishing conditions for human application. Success in a small animal alone does not establish transfer to humans.

Long-lead preparations run in parallel. This proposal starts designing human comparison data and longitudinal recordings in 2026–2030, then establishes feasible recording and replication arrangements in 2031–2035. Throughput and cost are reviewed from 2030 onward. This does not assume that all necessary whole-brain information can be acquired in a living person.

The technical foundation is the 2008 roadmap’s separation of scanning, interpretation and simulation, together with current measurement and fidelity research. These sources do not establish the dates above.[18][5][17]

Quantitative conditions for a 2050 scenario

The years are editorial review windows. The five-year spacing from 2030 to 2050 is a convenient cadence for revisiting dependent work, not a schedule calculated from growth rates or success probabilities. Meeting a gate permits consideration of the next integration; missing it extends both the workflow and timetable. Early stages establish measurement-error and individual-difference evidence before comparing measured throughput with later requirements.

Collins et al. (2025), Table 3, estimate human-brain imaging at about 34 years with 333 ExLSFM instruments or 1,667 years with 200 EM instruments, each representing US$100 million of instruments. Effective voxel sizes differ: 12.4×12.4×30 nm versus 3.6×3.6×45 nm. EM uses projected throughput; ExLSFM includes a hypothetical combination with expansion. Neither is demonstrated whole-brain operation.[17]

Keeping one fixed requires increasing the other by that factor. Procurement, construction and operating cost are not assumed to scale linearly.

Using all of 2046–2050 for imaging leaves no time for preparation, reconstruction, correction, modelling or independent validation. Budget each separately, specifying possible overlap and workflow dependencies. The mouse gate also requires automated tracing/correction at adequate accuracy. Change instrument counts and rates in the acquisition calculator.[17]

What the conditional integration target means

The target is a whole-brain model based on measurements of a particular person that corresponds to their activity, previously learned information, learning capacity and body interaction over prespecified functions and timescales. This is a technical research target, not certification of perfect reproduction in every situation. Individual memories and skills are part of the 2008 roadmap’s criteria; purpose-dependent tests draw on fidelity research.[18][5]

  • Traceable reconstruction: identify the individual, measured boundary, omissions and inferred information.
  • Individual correspondence: compare with average and other-individual models on held-out stimuli, interventions and memory tasks, without teaching the evaluation answers.
  • Continued operation: maintain function over specified periods and input/output conditions, including retention, forgetting and new learning.
  • Independent verification: enable replication by another team and report tolerances, cost, latency and untested scope.

Set numerical margins such as δ and ε in advance from biological repeats and the target function; see the validation procedure. These are proposed success criteria, not an existing certification standard.

The barriers that make 2050 demanding

1. Does sufficient information survive?

Well-preserved structure need not retain every state required for function. Experiments must connect preservation to memory tests.[10][11]

2. Can a map determine function?

Several models can fit the same observations. Compare unseen interventions to select the additional measurements that matter.[19][5]

3. Can the whole workflow run?

Imaging speed must be joined to tissue processing, tracing and correction, molecular measurements, modelling, execution and independent validation.[17][18]

Why the deadline is demanding: Collins et al.’s 2025 comparison suggests that even a human connectome project using expansion and light-sheet microscopy might become feasible roughly 25–50 years after 2025, conditional on further technical advances and with substantial uncertainty. This is the authors’ projection of technological readiness, not the duration of a single imaging run or a WBE completion forecast. Even the earlier end of this projection for this approach is around 2050. Requiring independent functional validation in that same year makes this proposal a more demanding stretch target. It would require faster acquisition/reconstruction and parallel evidence for function and memory.[17]

Acquisition-route assumption: this plan centres on routes that can include post-fixation structural measurements. A route that keeps the biological brain functioning while acquiring all necessary information requires an additional measurement capability. We do not assume that information lost during preservation can always be recovered later.[18][10]

Keep historical proposals in their context

Joseph Strout's Mind Uploading Home Page is an early web resource organizing scanning, computation, and replacement proposals. It displays a 2006 update date. It is useful historical context, but its resolution, cost, computing, and feasibility assumptions are not current evidence.[20]

Sandberg and Bostrom's 2008 technical roadmap organized preservation, scanning, interpretation, and simulation as dependencies. Its framework remains a reference point; quantitative estimates need reassessment against current measurements and methods.[18]

A sequence of technical advances

  1. 1952

    Quantifying electrical dynamics

    The Hodgkin–Huxley model provides a foundation for membrane-current modelling. It also illustrates the distance between modelling a cellular process and reproducing an individual organism.[21]

  2. 1986

    The foundational worm wiring diagram

    White et al.’s description of wiring in the C. elegans nervous system provided a foundation for later whole-animal connectomes and circuit studies. This historical attribution follows Cook et al. (2019), which updated the earlier work. Constructing a wiring diagram and validating a functioning model of an individual remain separate tasks.[22]

  3. 2008

    Organising WBE requirements and dependencies

    Sandberg and Bostrom’s roadmap organised the technical requirements linking preservation, scanning, interpretation and simulation. It provides a framework for separating stages, rather than establishing current instrument performance or a completion date.[18]

  4. 2014

    An open modelling programme linking nervous system and body

    The OpenWorm perspective described an integrated modelling programme and open research infrastructure for C. elegans. It documents the design and plans at that time, rather than reporting completed reproduction of all functions of an individual.[23]

  5. 2018

    A whole-brain EM image volume of an adult fly

    Zheng et al. released the FAFB EM image volume of one adult female fly brain and validated tracing in selected circuits. This was a milestone in whole-brain imaging, rather than a completed reconstruction of every neuron’s connections. FlyWire later built its reconstruction from these images.[24][1]

  6. 2019

    Whole-animal connectomes of both worm sexes

    Cook et al. presented connectomes of hermaphrodite and male C. elegans, spanning sensory inputs and outputs to muscles and other organs. They combine images from several animals with some extrapolation; they are not measurements of every state in one individual.[22]

  7. 2020

    Dense central-brain reconstruction and a mouse whole-brain proposal

    Scheffer et al.’s hemibrain densely reconstructed a large portion of one adult female fly’s central brain, linking about 25,000 neurons through about 20 million chemical synapses. It supports cell-type analysis, circuit studies and reuse, while remaining distinct from a completed whole-brain connectome or reproduction of function and memory.[25]

    In the same year, Abbott et al. proposed a synapse-level mouse whole-brain connectome as a resource for cell types, long-range wiring and individual variation. This commentary discusses automation, section loss, computation and cost; it does not report completed mouse whole-brain reconstruction or functional emulation.[26]

  8. 2023–2024

    Fly-brain connectomes and functional prediction

    The larval brain connectome and the adult FlyWire connectome reconstructed from FAFB became available. Connectome-constrained models also advanced functional prediction in the visual system. Structural coverage and the range of predicted functions require separate evaluation.[27][1][4]

  9. 2025

    Measuring and benchmarking correspondence

    MICrONS, LICONN, ZAPBench and fidelity research sharpened questions about structure–activity correspondence, reconstruction with molecular information, predictive benchmarking and evaluation for particular purposes.[2][28][9][5]

  10. 2026

    Linking brains with bodies

    Integrated datasets linking brain and ventral nerve cord advanced.[7][8] Project announcements of embodied models also prompted debate about calling them uploads. Official technical accounts, results tested in papers, and reproduction of an individual’s functions and memories need to be distinguished.[29][30]

Use reviews as entry points

State of Brain Emulation Report 2025 is a broad technical review. This guide uses the 182-page final report released in 2026; the title's “2025” is distinct from that release year. Projections and cost estimates should be separated from experimentally achieved results.[6]

Connect today’s work to the next gate

Carboncopies organises open questions through its roadmap update and develops measurement-to-model and evaluation infrastructure through BrainGenix and the Brain Emulation Challenge. Distinguish those officially described activities from this page’s 2050 plan.[31][32][16]

A concrete entry point for the 2030 stage is reproducing an existing connectome model and testing how reconstruction errors alter functional predictions. Track which gates gain evidence, alongside new publications.
