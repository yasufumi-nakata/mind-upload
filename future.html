Three research routes and a shared acquisition foundation

Work can begin with structure, activity or execution. Each route below moves from a published foundation to this site’s proposed experiment and the next test that success would make possible.

Route 1

Reconstruct from a connectome

Turn a brain map into a model that predicts.

  1. Evidence and research foundations

    Shiu et al. predicted sensorimotor functions using neuron-specific FlyWire connectivity. Lappalainen et al. combined local visual reconstructions into a consensus model sharing connections by cell type and relative position, optimized for motion estimation. Begin by reproducing one public model.[1][2][3]

  2. Next experiment

    Start by holding other quantities fixed and adding one connectivity-error type. For refitting, retain altered wiring and match training information, adjustable quantities and budgets across conditions. Define averaging and shuffles for each representation. Cross-individual comparisons require verified circuit, hemisphere and cell correspondence.

  3. Tests enabled by success

    Sensitivity curves can guide acquisition precision for the tested task and fixed/refit regime. Across representations and tasks, first test whether trends generalize. With activity from the same individual, test whether individual measurements explain responses better than an average model.

Evidence boundaries and branches if the test fails

Scope and constraints of the evidence

A decline with fixed parameters does not show that refitting cannot recover performance. Lappalainen already averages by type and relative position; neuron-specific errors require a derived model. Shared connectome coverage need not include all task inputs and outputs.[4][5]

Choose the next branch from the result

If the baseline fails to reproduce, check versions, inputs and preprocessing. Sensitivity to errors informs acquisition and proofreading requirements. Failure with fixed parameters can motivate a refit with separate evaluation data. If wiring does not explain differences, test functional, molecular or state measurements.

Start the comparison experiment

Route 2

Identify necessary information from activity

Test which measurements make a difference.

  1. Evidence and research foundations

    MICrONS pairs structure and activity in part of mouse visual cortex; ZAPBench compares activity prediction in larval zebrafish. Gonçalves et al. demonstrate inference of posterior distributions over neural-model parameters, rather than forcing a single parameter solution.[6][7][8]

  2. Next experiment

    In synthetic circuits with known truth, vary noise and recorded variables and check whether inferred ranges cover the truth. In biological data, compare predictions for unseen stimuli and interventions across models that fit the same recordings. Select measurements where predictions diverge, then test whether added data distinguish them. Use this route to identify G as well. First test observation conditions in artificial circuits with known rules, then constrain candidates with learning trajectories and compare predictions for held-out reward changes, extinction and reversal. Reproducible predictions support testing longer learning in route 3 with a body. See the procedure and evidence.

  3. Tests enabled by success

    If improvement persists on held-out data, it supports adding that measurement to acquisition. This helps choose between finer structure and additional molecular, activity or plasticity information, informing a plan to test individual memory.

Evidence boundaries and branches if the test fails

Scope and constraints of the evidence

MICrONS covers a local volume, and connectomes are not inputs to the ZAPBench evaluation. Posteriors depend on model and prior assumptions. Predictive agreement and identification of biological mechanisms require separate judgments.[9][5]

Choose the next branch from the result

If candidates remain indistinguishable, vary recording times or inputs to seek discriminating observations. If added measurements do not improve prediction, revisit the model class or evaluation task. The result still helps reassess measurement priorities and remaining uncertainty.

Route 3

Run models with a body and environment

Make circuit predictions work in an environment.

  1. Evidence and research foundations

    NEURON and Brain Modeling ToolKit (BMTK) provide neural-circuit simulation infrastructure. BAAIWorm links neurons, body and environment for a limited neural and behavioural scope. Local neural prostheses support evaluation of specific functions through recordings and interventions.[10][11][12][13][14]

  2. Next experiment

    First replay identical sensory inputs to compare circuit models. Then match body model, controller, initial conditions, environment and task in a closed loop where behaviour changes sensory feedback. Vary feedback delay, model state and learning one at a time. Separate comparisons holding non-circuit components fixed from comparisons that retune the controller; record permitted training information and budgets. Test after saving and resuming state. When adding learning, compare G candidates already tested on held-out data. Keep G fixed while allowing its weight updates; test correspondence to retention, forgetting and new learning in the same individual. Use failures to separate learning-rule errors from body-side causes.

  3. Tests enabled by success

    If circuit-derived improvements persist in the body–environment loop, they support integrating models obtained through measurement and identification. Compare retention, forgetting and new learning with data from the same individual, then extend the workflow to independent replication.

Evidence boundaries and branches if the test fails

Scope and constraints of the evidence

Performance supplied by a body controller or additional training must not be credited to circuit reproduction. Assisting a limited function with a neuroprosthesis does not establish gradual brain replacement or transfer of individual memories to another medium.[13][14][15]

Choose the next branch from the result

If behaviour breaks down, separate circuit prediction error, body control, sensory feedback and computational delay. Return to state measurements, or to reconstruction and acquisition/proofreading, as indicated; verify improvement on the same task.

Shared foundation for all three routes

Preserve and acquire the information models need

A shared acquisition foundation for all three routes.

  1. Evidence and research foundations

    FlyWire and MICrONS provide foundations for turning electron microscopy (EM) images into connectomes. aldehyde-stabilized cryopreservation (ASC) demonstrated preservation of fixed-tissue ultrastructure, and LICONN demonstrated optical reconstruction of local mammalian tissue. These are separate foundations for developing acquisition, not a demonstrated combined method.[1][6][16][17]

  2. Next experiment

    Begin with a fixed small public image volume. Compare alignment, segmentation, synapse detection and proofreading with independent references. Record splits, false merges, misses, proofreading person-hours, compute, transfer and storage. In specimen experiments, also test compatibility of preservation, expansion and molecular labels. Use accuracy requirements from the three routes to compare speed and cost per accepted original tissue volume.

  3. Tests enabled by success

    Acquiring required structural and molecular information at reproducible accuracy and cost supports modality selection and automation priorities. Next, expand the volume, verify correspondence across sections and sustained operation, and test delivery into a model of the same individual.

Scope, constraints and revisions after the experiment

Ultrastructure preservation does not demonstrate restored physiology or memory. Local LICONN results do not establish whole-human-brain acquisition. Fast imaging alone does not remove section loss, reconstruction errors or proofreading work. When required accuracy is unknown, report structural and model-functional evaluation separately.[18]

If accuracy is insufficient, change resolution, labels, preservation conditions or proofreading. Address local error concentrations first. If human effort keeps growing, evaluate automation before scaling volume. If added information has unclear value, return to activity and model comparisons to set measurement priorities.

Acquisition experiments and conditions

Converge on the same individual: connect information and test

These are this site’s proposed complementary research routes. Combine their requirements for structure, state and execution into a workflow that tests the original individual’s responses, memory and learning on held-out data.

Each experiment can inform the next measurement. A practical first step is to run one public model and compare one changed condition.

Revising the plan in response to research results

Working hypothesis: test whether structure and molecular information surviving fixation retain enough information about the long-term memories and individual differences of interest. Preserved anatomy and expert opinion do not establish sufficiency.[16][26]

  • If structure alone fails on intervention predictions, add functional or molecular observations and measure the benefit.
  • If individual wiring does not outperform an average model, inspect task sensitivity and model form. Report the possibility that average information is sufficient for the tested generic function.
  • If a method succeeds on artificial circuits but fails on biology, revise the synthetic-data assumptions.

Iterate between artificial circuits, small animals and local circuits, then test whether correspondence survives greater anatomical coverage. A sequence of species is not a guarantee or a timetable for human WBE.

Technical problems beyond computing speed

Compute is one requirement. Faster hardware cannot recover unmeasured information simply by running longer. Segmentation, registration, parameter identifiability, learning rules, and independent evaluation data remain linked challenges.[18][15][9]

Tracking demonstrated capabilities is more useful for research planning than assigning one arrival date. Extrapolation to humans must state differences in species, region, learning history, and observable timescales.
