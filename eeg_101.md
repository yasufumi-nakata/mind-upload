<div class="abstract-box">
<h2>TL;DR</h2>
<p>
EEG does not directly show the inside of the brain. It measures a mixed electrical pattern at the scalp. That gives EEG strong temporal resolution, but weak and blurry spatial localization. For that reason, Mind-Upload prioritizes data quality control (QC) and shared data-organization rules such as BIDS before anything else.
</p>
</div>

<div class="key-points">
<h4>Misreadings worth stopping early</h4>
<ul>
<li><strong>EEG is not magical mind reading:</strong> the observed signal is mixed, and the inverse problem has hard limits.</li>
<li><strong>But EEG is not useless:</strong> it is strong for temporal change and state transition tracking, so it is useful as part of a verification stack.</li>
<li><strong>Logs matter:</strong> without QC, synchronization, and preprocessing records, no one else can check the same result.</li>
<li><strong>Multimodal is not an escape hatch:</strong> same-session fusion, shared factors, and robust bundle evidence still need separate audits.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<div class="note-box">
<strong>When the same task label still hides a different measurement condition</strong>
<p>
This site previously stopped more clearly at <strong>subject / session fingerprint</strong> than at <strong>setup effects</strong>. That was too weak for EEG. <a href="https://doi.org/10.1088/1741-2552/aacfe4" target="_blank">Hu et al. (2018)</a> showed that reference montage and electrode setup alter the measured scalp potential itself, <a href="https://doi.org/10.3389/fnhum.2017.00150" target="_blank">Melnik et al. (2017)</a> showed that system, subject, and session each influence EEG recordings, <a href="https://doi.org/10.3389/fnhum.2020.00103" target="_blank">Xu et al. (2020)</a> showed that cross-dataset deep-learning results move with environmental variability such as amplifier, cap, sampling rate, and filtering, and <a href="https://doi.org/10.1016/j.brainresbull.2024.110906" target="_blank">Dong et al. (2024)</a> showed that comparative work across different channel locations needs explicit harmonization. Therefore, on this site, <strong>site / device / reference system / electrode layout / protocol</strong> are treated as part of the measurement condition. A score that does not report them is not read as clean evidence that only the target neural variable changed.
</p>
</div>

<div class="note-box">
<strong>When a large pretrained EEG model looks like a general decoder</strong>
<p>
This site does not read a foundation-model headline through score alone. <a href="https://doi.org/10.3389/fnhum.2021.653659" target="_blank">Kostas et al. (2021)</a> already framed transfer across differing hardware, subjects, and tasks as the core challenge, <a href="https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2024/file/47393e8594c82ce8fd83adc672cf9872-Paper-Conference.pdf" target="_blank">Jiang et al. (2024)</a> explicitly listed electrode mismatch, unequal sample length, varied task design, and low SNR as still-open EEG barriers, <a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.21585" target="_blank">El Ouahidi et al. (2025)</a> pushed toward setup-agnostic pretraining over more than <strong>60,000 hours</strong> and <strong>92 datasets</strong>, <a href="https://proceedings.mlr.press/v267/lee25a.html" target="_blank">Lee et al. (2025)</a> found only marginal gains over conventional deep baselines under fine-tuning, <a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.17742" target="_blank">Xiong et al. (2025)</a> argued that inconsistent evaluation protocols make cross-model comparison unreliable, <a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.02268" target="_blank">Lahiri et al. (2026)</a> showed that narrow-source and diverse-source pretraining can trade places across linear-probe and fine-tuning regimes, and <a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.17883" target="_blank">Liu et al. (2026)</a> showed that linear probing is often insufficient and specialist-from-scratch models remain competitive. Therefore, on this site, large-model EEG results are read through the <a href="wiki/eeg-foundation-models.html#pretraining-card">Wiki: Pretraining Card</a> and <a href="verification.html#pretraining-card">Verification: Pretraining Card</a>. If the downstream claim is about a neural variable rather than just benchmark transfer, they must also pass the <a href="verification.html#specificity-shortcut-card">Specificity &amp; Shortcut Card</a>, because "works across setups" is still not the same as "stopped reading identity / setup shortcuts."
</p>
</div>

<div class="note-box">
<strong>Benchmark operations are part of the result, not administrative detail</strong>
<p>
The official <a href="https://eeg2025.github.io/" target="_blank">EEG Challenge 2025 homepage</a> states that the challenge paper is already out of date relative to execution-phase changes. The official <a href="https://eeg2025.github.io/submission/" target="_blank">submission page</a> fixes the event as an <strong>inference-only code competition</strong>, while the <a href="https://eeg2025.github.io/rules/" target="_blank">rules page</a> requires disclosure of extra pretraining datasets, pretrained checkpoints, fine-tuning method, and a <strong>single-GPU 20 GB</strong> inference-stage budget. The final <a href="https://eeg2025.github.io/leaderboard/" target="_blank">leaderboard</a> then disclosed that Challenge 2 samples had not been randomized, which allowed some teams to exploit contiguous-trial same-subject structure and led organizers to split the awards. On this site, that means <strong>benchmark provenance is part of the evidence</strong>: split construction, hidden grouping, checkpoint policy, normalization, inference budget, and later postmortems all change what a leaderboard score is allowed to mean.
</p>
</div>

<div class="note-box">
<strong>Metric semantics are part of the EEG claim, not a post-processing detail</strong>
<p>
This page had already separated <strong>split design</strong>, <strong>benchmark provenance</strong>, and <strong>calibration</strong>, but it still left one weak shortcut too implicit: <strong>what score is allowed to summarize the task</strong>. The primary literature does not support that shortcut. <a href="https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0118432" target="_blank">Saito &amp; Rehmsmeier (2015)</a> showed that <strong>precision-recall</strong> views are often more informative than ROC summaries under strong class imbalance. In seizure tasks, <a href="https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ebiom.2021.103275" target="_blank">Roy et al. (2021)</a>, <a href="https://doi.org/10.1097/WNP.0000000000000709" target="_blank">Scheuer et al. (2021)</a>, and <a href="https://doi.org/10.3389/fnins.2023.1184990" target="_blank">Segal et al. (2023)</a> show that practical value turns on <strong>sensitivity</strong>, <strong>false alarms</strong>, <strong>event-overlap rules</strong>, <strong>latency</strong>, and sometimes <strong>threshold / calibration control</strong> rather than one headline number. In sleep staging, <a href="https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/zsx139" target="_blank">Sun et al. (2017)</a>, <a href="https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.70092" target="_blank">Vallat &amp; Walker (2021)</a>, and <a href="https://doi.org/10.1038/s41746-025-02237-2" target="_blank">Dei Rossi et al. (2026)</a> show that <strong>Cohen's kappa</strong>, <strong>macro-F1</strong>, <strong>per-stage performance</strong>, and <strong>bias / out-of-domain slices</strong> answer different questions. Therefore, on this site, <strong>accuracy</strong> or <strong>AUROC</strong> alone are not accepted as a generic EEG headline when class balance, event cost, or stage structure matters.
</p>
</div>

<section class="section" id="what-is-eeg">
<h2 class="section-title">What EEG is, very roughly</h2>
<p>
EEG records voltage differences using electrodes placed on the scalp. What is observed is the result of many neural activities mixed together and filtered through tissue, skull, and scalp. EEG can measure rapidly, at millisecond scale, but the signal is spatially blurred and therefore easy to overinterpret if the limits are ignored.
</p>
</section>

<div class="note-box">
<strong>Typical ways “the same EEG” yields different results</strong>
<p>
Changing the <strong>reference scheme</strong> changes the apparent scalp distribution. Changing <strong>electrode positions or channel layout</strong> changes which field patterns are even sampled. Changing <strong>filter settings</strong> or the <strong>device-side chain</strong> changes which slow or fast components remain. Tightening <strong>artifact removal</strong> may also remove neural signal you wanted to preserve. That is why results cannot be compared unless the acquisition condition and processing path are recorded together with the result.
</p>
</div>

<section class="section" id="information-theoretic-limits">
<h2 class="section-title">Information-theoretic limits of EEG and what they imply for WBE</h2>
<p>
To place EEG inside the context of WBE, it is useful to distinguish between constraints that technology may improve and limits that follow more fundamentally from physics and mathematics.
</p>

<div class="key-points">
<h4>The gap in spatial resolution</h4>
<ul>
<li><strong>What scalp EEG directly gives you:</strong> a mixed voltage distribution on the scalp. Even when source estimation is used, effective resolution depends strongly on electrode density, SNR, head-model quality, and electrode-coordinate error.</li>
<li><strong>What high-density EEG improves:</strong> constraints on broad activity near the cortical surface become better, but deep sources and fine-grained local circuits still cannot be uniquely reconstructed.</li>
<li><strong>The gap to WBE:</strong> if WBE required preservation of cellular, synaptic, and local-circuit dynamics, scalp EEG would still be orders of magnitude too coarse. More sensors alone do not close that gap.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<div class="key-points">
<h4>Bandwidth constraints</h4>
<ul>
<li><strong>Sensor count is not the same as independent information:</strong> even at 256 channels, volume conduction and spatial correlation reduce the independent degrees of freedom far below raw channel count.</li>
<li><strong>EEG observes a low-dimensional projection:</strong> it reflects large-scale brain dynamics, but it does not carry the full detail of microscopic circuit structure.</li>
<li><strong>So its role is necessarily limited:</strong> EEG is useful for state estimation, transition detection, closed-loop control, and constraining other modalities, but not for reconstructing the entire circuit by itself.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<div class="key-points">
<h4>Fundamental physical and mathematical constraints</h4>
<ul>
<li><strong>Field formation and volume conduction:</strong> from intracranial current source to scalp, skull, cerebrospinal fluid, and scalp, the signal is not only low-pass filtered but also reshaped by source extent, orientation, cortical folding, and cancellation. This is a physical limitation, not something removed by better hardware alone.</li>
<li><strong>Ill-posed inverse problem:</strong> multiple different internal source configurations can generate the same scalp potential pattern. In principle, scalp EEG cannot uniquely determine the source configuration (Hämäläinen &amp; Ilmoniemi, 1994). Prior information such as structural MRI, fMRI, or anatomical constraints is required.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<div class="note-box">
<strong>Before the inverse problem, there is already a field-formation visibility wall</strong>
<p>
Another weak reading to stop early is to think that all important neural events are already present at the scalp and that source imaging only has to recover them algorithmically. The primary literature does not support that shortcut. <a href="https://doi.org/10.1007/s10548-010-0154-x" target="_blank">Ahlfors et al. (2010)</a> quantified source-orientation sensitivity with realistic boundaries and found that the median ratio between the least and most sensitive orientations was <strong>0.63 for EEG</strong> but only <strong>0.06 for MEG</strong>. <a href="https://doi.org/10.1002/hbm.20851" target="_blank">Ahlfors et al. (2010)</a> also showed that extended and distributed sources can cancel substantially at the surface. <a href="https://doi.org/10.1002/hbm.20571" target="_blank">Goldenholz et al. (2009)</a> then showed that source extent and anatomy matter strongly for detectability: in mesial temporal cortex, simulated source patches of about <strong>3 cm<sup>2</sup></strong> versus <strong>8 cm<sup>2</sup></strong> differed by roughly <strong>10 dB</strong> in SNR, and the modality preference changed across cortex rather than staying fixed. <a href="https://doi.org/10.1002/hbm.25272" target="_blank">Piastra et al. (2021)</a> further showed with detailed FEM models that ignoring the <strong>CSF compartment</strong> overestimates EEG SNR and that cortical / subcortical sensitivity depends jointly on depth and orientation. Therefore, what reaches the scalp is already a <strong>geometry- and tissue-filtered subset</strong> of brain activity. More channels or a more sophisticated inverse solver cannot recreate source detail that field formation never delivered to the sensors.
</p>
</div>

<div class="note-box">
<strong>Conclusion: EEG alone cannot provide enough information for WBE</strong>
<p>
The role of EEG is to provide <strong>macroscopic constraints</strong> on brain dynamics. Concretely, that includes:
</p>
<ul>
<li>Global-state calibration such as wakefulness, sleep, or anesthesia</li>
<li>Consciousness monitoring such as PCI-style measures</li>
<li>Longitudinal stability tracking, separating what changes from what remains stable over hours or days</li>
</ul>
<p>
If the target is WBE, EEG belongs as one macroscopic modality that complements invasive recordings, electron microscopy, molecular profiling, and other richer measurements.
</p>
</div>

<div class="note-box">
<strong>Do not underrate it either: under strict conditions, limited deep inference exists</strong>
<p>
It would also be inaccurate to say “deep sources are completely invisible.” Studies combining high-density EEG (256 channels), individual MRI, realistic head models, and simultaneous intracranial recording have reported significant correspondence between scalp-EEG source images and signals near the thalamus or nucleus accumbens (Seeber et al., 2019). <a href="https://doi.org/10.1002/hbm.26720" target="_blank">Afnan et al. (2024)</a> further showed that <strong>depth-weighted cMEM</strong> improved localization of deep generators, especially mesial temporal sources, across <strong>5400 realistic simulations</strong> and epilepsy patient data. But in direct validation using intracranial electrical stimulation as ground truth, even 37-electrode scalp EEG still showed mean localization errors of <strong>10.3-26 mm</strong> depending on source depth and skull-conductivity optimization (Unnwongse et al., 2023). The right summary is therefore not “impossible,” but rather “limited inference is possible under tightly fixed conditions, and the error depends strongly on source class, source depth, SNR, and conductivity assumptions.”
</p>
</div>

<div class="note-box">
<strong>As of 2026-03, what should count as “improvement”?</strong>
<p>
Source-imaging improvement is not established merely by increasing channel count. At minimum, one should show <strong>a forward model built from individual MRI or equivalent geometry</strong>, <strong>a visibility argument that the targeted source class is expected to reach the scalp given its depth / orientation / extent / cancellation profile</strong>, <strong>a posterior distribution or confidence interval</strong>, <strong>sensitivity analysis of the forward and tissue model</strong>, and <strong>validation against a named validation class such as simulation / phantom, intracranial stimulation, simultaneous invasive recording, or postsurgical outcome</strong>. Improvement means not “we can now see deep structures,” but “a third party can audit under which conditions the error was reduced, by how much, for which source class, and on which benchmark family.”
</p>
</div>

<section class="section" id="claim-audit">
<h2 class="section-title">2026-03 literature audit: EEG claims are cut by four gates</h2>
<p>
The key weakness we needed to tighten was the temptation to read EEG progress from a single score or a single source map. When you line up the primary literature, at least <strong>observability</strong>, <strong>specificity</strong>, <strong>identifiability</strong>, and <strong>deployability</strong> are separate audits. The facts that <strong>some information is present in the scalp signal</strong>, <strong>that the information comes from the target neural variable</strong>, <strong>that the internal source or network has been narrowed toward a unique solution</strong>, and <strong>that performance survives across days or in closed loop</strong> are not supported by the same evidence.
</p>
<table class="data-table">
<thead>
<tr>
<th>gate</th>
<th>Question asked here</th>
<th>Minimum evidence wanted</th>
<th>What this gate still cannot claim</th>
</tr>
</thead>
<tbody>
<tr>
<td><strong>observability</strong></td>
<td>Is information about the target state or task condition actually present in scalp EEG?</td>
<td>Synchronized event logs, an appropriate hold-out, simultaneous recording or an external reference, and decoding / tracking with artifact audit.</td>
<td>It still cannot claim source uniqueness, general deep-structure recovery, causal network structure, or long-term operational viability.</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td><strong>specificity</strong></td>
<td>Is the score reading the target neural variable, or is it reading shortcuts such as eye movement, EMG, uninstructed movement, feedback, or subject fingerprint?</td>
<td>EOG / EMG / behavior logs, nuisance-only baselines, countermeasures such as fixed gaze or feedback-off, and slice-wise hold-outs.</td>
<td>You still cannot write "the score is high, therefore it is a neural readout" or "the class appeared, therefore the internal state was understood."</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td><strong>identifiability</strong></td>
<td>How narrowly and uniquely can internal sources or networks be constrained from the observation?</td>
<td>Individual MRI, measured electrode coordinates, the forward model, conductivity assumptions, uncertainty display, and external validation via phantom, intracranial stimulation, or simultaneous invasive recording.</td>
<td>You still cannot write "the visible source is the true unique solution" or "the network diagram is the circuit diagram."</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td><strong>deployability</strong></td>
<td>How well does performance survive across days, reattachment, long-term drift, and closed-loop use?</td>
<td>Cross-session / cross-subject evaluation, a fixed decoder interval, recalibration burden, and disclosed latency, jitter, and abstention behavior.</td>
<td>You still cannot rephrase a high same-day score as real-world stability or WBE-relevant sustained constraint.</td>
</tr>
</tbody>
</table>

<div class="note-box">
<strong>Site rule from this 4-way split</strong>
<p>
Results that pass observability are described only as <strong>"the signal contains information"</strong>. Only after specificity evidence is added do we write that <strong>"the information is relatively specific to the target neural variable"</strong>. Only after identifiability evidence is added do we write that <strong>"a source / network was estimated"</strong>. Only after deployability evidence is added do we write that <strong>"the method may survive across days, long-term use, or closed loop."</strong> This is how the boundaries drawn by H&auml;m&auml;l&auml;inen &amp; Ilmoniemi (1994), Mostert et al. (2018), Musall et al. (2019), Ma et al. (2022), and Wilson et al. (2025) are turned into operational reading rules on this site.
</p>
</div>
<div class="note-box">
<strong>2026-03-18 addendum: "something was visible" is not the same as "the target was visible"</strong>
<p>
<a href="https://doi.org/10.1523/ENEURO.0401-17.2018" target="_blank">Mostert et al. (2018)</a> showed that visual-working-memory decoding can retain an eye-movement confound, <a href="https://doi.org/10.3389/fnhum.2013.00138" target="_blank">Muthukumaraswamy (2013)</a> summarized how high-frequency power overlaps with muscle artifact, <a href="https://doi.org/10.1088/1741-2560/2/4/014" target="_blank">McFarland et al. (2005)</a> showed that EMG can boost early BCI-session performance, and <a href="https://doi.org/10.1038/s42256-024-00837-5" target="_blank">Chen et al. (2024)</a> showed that post-onset auditory feedback can inflate offline speech-decoding scores. In addition, <a href="https://doi.org/10.1038/s41746-019-0178-x" target="_blank">Chaibub Neto et al. (2019)</a> showed that diagnostic models can learn identity-confounding signals when repeated measures are not participant-disjoint, while <a href="https://doi.org/10.1016/j.patcog.2020.107381" target="_blank">Wang et al. (2020)</a> and <a href="https://doi.org/10.3389/fnhum.2021.672946" target="_blank">Di et al. (2021)</a> showed that resting-state EEG can support time-robust person identification. For that reason, decode / biomarker results on this site are paired with the <a href="verification.html#specificity-shortcut-card">Verification: Specificity &amp; Shortcut Card</a> so the target path and shortcut paths are audited separately.
</p>
</div>
</section>

<section class="section" id="generalization-gates">
<h2 class="section-title">The same decoding score can support very different claims</h2>
<p>
One of the most common EEG misreadings is to take <strong>same-day / same-subject separation</strong> and silently rephrase it as <strong>stable across days</strong>, <strong>generalized to other people</strong>, or <strong>deployable long term</strong>. The MOABB documentation itself separates `WithinSessionEvaluation`, `CrossSessionEvaluation`, and `CrossSubjectEvaluation` because they are different scientific questions.
</p>

<table class="data-table">
<thead>
<tr>
<th>Evaluation family</th>
<th>What it mainly tests</th>
<th>What it still cannot say</th>
</tr>
</thead>
<tbody>
<tr>
<td><strong>within-session</strong></td>
<td>How well task and class separate on the same day and setup.</td>
<td>It does not establish cross-day stability, tolerance to reattachment, or long-term drift robustness.</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td><strong>cross-session</strong></td>
<td>How well features survive across days for the same person, despite state variation and setup change.</td>
<td>It does not establish subject-independent generalization or the absence of recalibration requirements.</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td><strong>cross-subject</strong></td>
<td>Whether some structure generalizes across people and can support a cold-start route.</td>
<td>It does not establish the same strength of claim as a decoder adapted to one individual, nor a personal long-term controller.</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td><strong>longitudinal / closed loop</strong></td>
<td>How many days a fixed decoder survives, plus recalibration burden, latency, and silence/abstention behavior.</td>
<td>Offline accuracy alone is not enough to call it a deployable loop.</td>
</tr>
</tbody>
</table>

<div class="note-box">
<strong>What the primary literature actually shows</strong>
<p>
In the five-day MI dataset from Ma et al. (2022), subject-specific mean accuracy fell from <strong>68.8%</strong> within-session to <strong>53.7%</strong> cross-session, then rose to <strong>78.9%</strong> with adaptation using a small amount of target-session data. Same-day score and cross-day durability are therefore not the same achievement. Musall et al. (2019) further showed that neural dynamics during task can be strongly shaped by uninstructed movement, and Wilson et al. (2025) showed that long-term BCI operation requires <strong>frequent recalibration</strong> because activity patterns themselves drift over time.
</p>
</div>

<div class="note-box">
<strong>A safe reading rule for this page</strong>
<p>
When you see a high EEG score, check at least <strong>(1) what was held out</strong>, <strong>(2) how eye movement, muscle activity, and uninstructed movement were audited</strong>, <strong>(3) whether target-day or target-subject data were used</strong>, and <strong>(4) how many days a fixed decoder was held</strong>. If these are missing, this site reads the result only as a <strong>qualified decode</strong>, not as WBE-relevant state reconstruction or a deployable loop.
</p>
</div>
</section>

<section class="section" id="preprocessing-acceptance-gates">
<h2 class="section-title">2026-03 literature audit: preprocessing is not cosmetic, it is an acceptance gate</h2>
<p>
EEG preprocessing is not the stage where you merely make the later figures look better. It is the auditing step where you decide which parts of the signal are treated as neural and which claims must still be stopped. COBIDAS-MEEG and EEG-BIDS define a concrete reporting floor. The PREP pipeline shows that bad-channel handling and rereferencing cannot be separated cleanly, Widmann and colleagues show that filter design can distort waveform shape and latency, and recent decoding work shows that artifact correction does not always improve performance. For that reason, this site treats preprocessing as an <strong>acceptance gate</strong>, not as preference tuning.
</p>

<strong>Site rule derived from this audit</strong>
<p>

The practical details are centralized in <a href="wiki/eeg-preprocessing-and-qc.html">Wiki: EEG preprocessing and QC</a>.
</p>
</div>
<div class="note-box">
<strong>Connectivity metrics are not leak-proof or causal just by name</strong>
<p>
Artifact-cleaning and line-noise tools such as <a href="https://doi.org/10.1109/EMBC.2018.8512547" target="_blank">ASR</a> and <a href="https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuroimage.2019.116356" target="_blank">ZapLine</a> help with reproducible cleanup, but they do not solve <strong>volume conduction</strong>, <strong>source leakage</strong>, or <strong>directional identifiability</strong>. <a href="https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuroimage.2011.01.055" target="_blank">Vinck et al. (2011)</a> made wPLI safer than coherence or PLV against some zero-lag mixing, yet <a href="https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuroimage.2012.09.036" target="_blank">Haufe et al. (2013)</a> showed that sensor-space connectivity remains severely limited by volume conduction, <a href="https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuroimage.2018.02.032" target="_blank">Palva et al. (2018)</a> showed that even leakage-insensitive source-space measures can yield ghost interactions, and <a href="https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neures.2019.09.003" target="_blank">Ye et al. (2020)</a> evaluated STE under TMS precisely because causality is difficult to identify from observations alone. Therefore, on this site, <strong>wPLI, source-space connectivity, and STE are read as auditable estimators with explicit ceilings</strong>, not as leak-proof or causal readouts by naming convention.
</p>
</div>

<section class="section" id="esi-uncertainty">
<h2 class="section-title">What must now be stated rigorously about ESI</h2>
<p>
The key point in this section is not which algorithm name was used, but <strong>what chain of evidence was used to audit error</strong>. EEG source imaging is easy to overrate if future promise is confused with the minimum evidence required today.
</p>

<div class="key-points">
<h4>Five floors of evidence you should disclose</h4>
<ul>
<li><strong>Visibility floor:</strong> before inverse choice, disclose why the targeted generator class should reach the scalp at all, naming source depth, orientation, extent, cancellation risk, and whether CSF-aware head modeling was used. A better solver cannot recover a source that field formation filtered below usable SNR.</li>
<li><strong>Reporting floor:</strong> under COBIDAS-MEEG and EEG-BIDS, publish the reference scheme, <code>SamplingFrequency</code>, <code>SoftwareFilters</code>, bad segments, preprocessing conditions, electrode coordinates, and coordinate system. BIDS is necessary but is not itself ground truth.</li>
<li><strong>Geometry floor:</strong> if making an anatomical source claim, disclose individual MRI, measured electrode positions, the forward model, and assumptions about skull conductivity. Template MRI can substitute, but geometry and conductivity mismatch still widen error (Birot et al., 2014; Aydin et al., 2019).</li>
<li><strong>Validation floor:</strong> report localization error or concordance against a named validation class such as simulation / phantom, intracranial stimulation, simultaneous SEEG/HD-EEG, or a clinical cohort with postsurgical outcome. A point estimate alone is not enough, and “externally validated” is too vague.</li>
<li><strong>Solver-stability floor:</strong> if standard inverse families or reasonable parameter windows give materially different source locations, report the spread. A single best-looking map is not enough by itself.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<div class="key-points">
<h4>Separate what is required from what is promising</h4>
<ul>
<li><strong>Required:</strong> a described forward model, electrode coregistration, preprocessing logs, error metrics, published failure conditions, and either posterior / ensemble width or cross-solver / cross-parameter sensitivity.</li>
<li><strong>Promising candidates:</strong> Bayesian uncertainty maps, Champagne-family methods, and Bayesian skull-conductivity modeling are all strong options, but as of 2026-03 none can yet be called the single standard answer.</li>
<li><strong>What should not be frozen into a recipe:</strong> Michel &amp; Brunet (2019) explicitly warn against relying blindly on automatic artifact removal and recommend visual inspection alongside it. Therefore ASR and ZapLine-plus are better treated as candidates to be audited with sensitivity analysis, not as universal mandatory steps.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<div class="note-box">
<strong>2026-03-19 addendum: a single best inverse map is not enough</strong>
<p>
The remaining weak point was to let one clean source map stand in for the whole solution set. <a href="https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuroimage.2017.02.076" target="_blank">Mahjoory et al. (2017)</a> showed that inverse-method and software-package choice can materially change EEG source localization and especially downstream connectivity, and explicitly recommended verifying results with more than one source-imaging procedure. <a href="https://doi.org/10.1038/s41597-020-0467-x" target="_blank">Mikulan et al. (2020)</a> then used known intracranial stimulation sites and showed that, when all tested parameter combinations were considered, localization distances in the benchmark commonly ranged from about 2 mm to 50 mm rather than collapsing to one stable answer. <a href="https://doi.org/10.3389/fnhum.2024.1335212" target="_blank">Vorwerk et al. (2024)</a> further showed that tissue-conductivity uncertainty, especially skull conductivity, can shift reconstructed depth and location. On this site, a single best-looking inverse map is therefore not read as stable anatomical evidence unless the paper also shows <strong>cross-solver / cross-parameter spread</strong> or a <strong>posterior / ensemble uncertainty width</strong>.
</p>
</div>
<div class="note-box">
<strong>2026-03-27 addendum: conditional deep-source gains are not a general deep-coverage license</strong>
<p>
The next shortcut to block is to compress every recent deep-source paper into one impression that “deep recovery is now basically solved if the pipeline is careful enough.” The primary literature does not support that reading. <a href="https://doi.org/10.1002/hbm.26720" target="_blank">Afnan et al. (2024)</a> improved deep-generator localization, especially mesial temporal sources, across <strong>5400 realistic simulations</strong> and epilepsy patient data, but that paper is still a <strong>conditional deep-epilepsy route</strong> rather than a generic proof of whole-brain deep recoverability. <a href="https://doi.org/10.1111/epi.18552" target="_blank">Hao et al. (2025)</a> then showed in <strong>29 simultaneous HD-EEG/SEEG cases</strong> that ictal ESI remained at <strong>14.07 &plusmn; 4.62 mm</strong> versus <strong>17.38 &plusmn; 4.16 mm</strong> for interictal ESI, with <strong>source depth</strong> and <strong>spike power</strong> still materially affecting accuracy. <a href="https://doi.org/10.1088/1741-2552/ae2f01" target="_blank">Vorwerk et al. (2026)</a> further showed that individualized skull-conductivity estimation can reduce source-localization uncertainty from <strong>a few centimeters to less than one centimeter for most simulated sources</strong>, while explicitly finding that <strong>sources at the base of the brain remain less improved</strong> and that point estimates still hide residual uncertainty. On this site, those papers are therefore read as <strong>source-class- and benchmark-conditioned error reduction</strong>, not as generic proof that scalp EEG now observes arbitrary deep generators.
</p>
</div>

<div class="key-points">
<h4>The upper bound shown by current measured evidence</h4>
<ul>
<li><strong>Field formation already removes information:</strong> orientation, folding, source extent, and cancellation decide which generators reach the scalp with usable SNR before inversion even starts.</li>
<li><strong>Deep detection works only under explicit conditions:</strong> Seeber et al. (2019) used 256-channel scalp EEG with simultaneous DBS recording and found correspondence near thalamic and nucleus-accumbens regions. That does not mean deep sources are uniquely recoverable in general.</li>
<li><strong>Stimulation-ground-truth error still spreads with assumptions:</strong> Unnwongse et al. (2023) used intracranial stimulation as ground truth and showed mean localization errors ranging from 10.3 to 26.0 mm depending on source depth and skull-conductivity setting.</li>
<li><strong>Concurrent invasive reference still leaves sizable error:</strong> Hao et al. (2025) reported mean localization errors of 14.07 +/- 4.62 mm for ictal ESI and 17.38 +/- 4.16 mm for interictal ESI in simultaneous HD-EEG/SEEG, with depth and source power strongly affecting performance.</li>
<li><strong>Conductivity calibration helps but does not erase the wall:</strong> Vorwerk et al. (2026) reduced localization uncertainty for many simulated sources, yet basal sources remained less improved and residual uncertainty still needed explicit treatment.</li>
<li><strong>Validation class is part of the claim:</strong> stimulation ground truth, simultaneous invasive recording, and postsurgical outcome do not answer the same error question. On this site, their benchmark class must be named before the claim ceiling is raised.</li>
<li><strong>Even in clinical use, overconfidence is unsafe:</strong> In the E-PILEPSY systematic review by Mouthaan et al. (2019), source imaging had summary sensitivity of 82% and specificity of 53%, but all included studies still carried some form of bias. Even in a relatively structured presurgical setting, source imaging remains decision support, not ground truth.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<div class="note-box">
<strong>Conclusion of this page</strong>
<p>
If you want to claim improvement in ESI, the first question is not “which method did you use?” but <strong>on which benchmark, for which error, and by how much was that error reduced?</strong> Claims about source imaging should not be closed inside public starter datasets alone. They should be read together with <a href="datasets.html#validation-ladder">Datasets: The source-imaging validation ladder</a>.
</p>
</div>
</section>

<section class="section" id="multimodal-integration">
<h2 class="section-title">Multimodal integration is not a shortcut; it is another audit problem</h2>
<p>
It is not enough to say that EEG alone is insufficient and therefore adding other modalities automatically solves the problem. Integration is useful, but it also <strong>adds each modality's own error sources</strong>.
</p>
<div class="key-points">
<h4>Roles and constraints by integration type</h4>
<ul>
<li><strong>EEG + fMRI:</strong> the complementarity across time and space is attractive, but simultaneous recording adds artifacts and safety constraints that depend on field strength. As Jorge et al. (2015) shows, at 7T even cable length and placement can materially change noise, and the hemodynamic side still needs vascular-state / CVR interpretation rather than “just add spatial resolution.”</li>
<li><strong>EEG + MEG:</strong> the sensitivity profiles are complementary, and Aydin et al. (2014) showed that combined EEG/MEG with realistic head models and conductivity calibration can improve reliability. But that advantage depends on the quality of skull-conductivity calibration and coregistration.</li>
<li><strong>EEG + PET + MRI:</strong> simultaneous acquisition can compare electrophysiology, hemodynamics, and metabolism under one window, but PET quantification, vascular interpretation, shared-factor specificity, and bundle robustness still remain explicit audit items.</li>
<li><strong>OPM-MEG:</strong> Boto et al. (2018) showed recording feasibility during natural movement, but also showed that without background field nulling, sensor saturation under head movement is a real risk. OPM-MEG is promising, but not yet a simple drop-in replacement.</li>
</ul>
</div>
<div class="note-box">
<strong>Same-session multimodal EEG still needs a Fusion Card</strong>
<p>
The older short rule on this page was still too weak because it let <strong>simultaneous</strong> or <strong>multimodal</strong> sound closer to self-validating than the primary literature supports. <a href="https://doi.org/10.1162/IMAG.a.136" target="_blank">Kothe et al. (2025)</a> describe LSL as synchronization infrastructure rather than device-side delay truth, <a href="https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuroimage.2020.116595" target="_blank">Wei et al. (2020)</a> treat EEG-fMRI fusion as a model-conditioned inference problem, <a href="https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-023-44363-z" target="_blank">Vafaii et al. (2024)</a> show that simultaneous multimodal recordings retain both common and divergent organization, and <a href="https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-025-64414-x" target="_blank">Chen et al. (2025)</a> show coupled global dynamics together with modality- and network-specific structure in simultaneous EEG-PET-MRI across wakefulness and NREM sleep. Therefore, even same-session multimodal EEG results still need a <a href="verification.html#fusion-card">Fusion Card</a> before they are read above the strongest unimodal or prior-conditioned ceiling.
</p>
</div>
<div class="note-box">
<strong>A shared multimodal factor can still be mixed physiology</strong>
<p>
Even when a paper reports a strong common component, that component is not yet automatically the target neural variable. <a href="https://doi.org/10.1162/imag_a_00287" target="_blank">Gold et al. (2024)</a> show that fMRI-autonomic covariance grows as vigilance decreases in simultaneous EEG-fMRI-autonomic recordings, <a href="https://doi.org/10.1038/s42003-019-0659-0" target="_blank">Özbay et al. (2019)</a> show that sympathetic activity contributes to the fMRI signal during EEG-marked arousal events, and <a href="https://doi.org/10.1038/s41593-025-02132-9" target="_blank">Epp et al. (2025)</a> show that significant BOLD changes can oppose oxygen-metabolism changes across about 40% of task-responsive cortical voxels. On this site, a shared EEG-fMRI or EEG-PET-MRI factor therefore has to be labeled as a <strong>shared neural candidate</strong>, a <strong>physiology-linked global factor</strong>, or <strong>mixed / unresolved</strong> rather than being promoted directly to one solved state variable.
</p>
</div>
<div class="note-box">
<strong>More modalities do not make the bundle robust by default</strong>
<p>
Bundle-level performance gains are real, but they still need their own stop lines. <a href="https://doi.org/10.1038/s41591-024-03019-1" target="_blank">Rohaut et al. (2024)</a> show that multimodal assessment can improve neuroprognosis while reducing uncertain cases in severe brain injury. But <a href="https://doi.org/10.1093/brain/awac335" target="_blank">Amiri et al. (2023)</a> treated direct same-sample multimodal prediction as a separate analysis inside an acute DoC cohort, and <a href="https://doi.org/10.1093/brain/awaf412" target="_blank">Manasova et al. (2026)</a> report cross-centre evaluation together with missing-value substitution and higher inter-modality disagreement in clinically hard groups. Therefore, this site does not read ``more modalities improved performance'' as if the bundle were already availability-agnostic, transfer-stable, or coherent in the hardest regimes.
</p>
</div>
<div class="note-box">
<strong>Implementation rule</strong>
<p>
The value of integration is not that “more modalities were used.” The value lies in recording coordinate-alignment error, delay mismatch, noise-structure differences, shared-vs-specific component logic, availability / complete-case slice, and uncertainty propagation separately, then showing which specific errors were reduced. Even after integration, each modality's raw data and metadata should still remain individually auditable. If the bundle also mixes living-human proxy classes such as PET, MRSI, or support-state routes, this page also routes it to the <a href="verification.html#human-proxy-composition-card">Human Proxy Composition Card</a> rather than letting the word <strong>multimodal</strong> carry that burden implicitly.
</p>
</div>
<div class="note-box">
<strong>When you want only the entry-level picture first</strong>
<p>
If you want a basic explanation of why EEG, MEG, fMRI, ECoG, and MRI are combined at all, read <a href="wiki/multimodal-integration-basics.html">Wiki: Basics of multimodal integration</a> first, then come back to this section.
</p>
</div>
</section>
