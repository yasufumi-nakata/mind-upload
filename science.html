What must we measure to reproduce a brain?

Connectivity, cellular responses, plasticity, molecular state, and interaction with a body constrain different aspects of a model.

Glossary

connectome
Data describing neurons and their connections within a specified boundary. Report cell, region and chemical/electrical-synapse coverage, omissions and errors separately; the term does not guarantee completeness or functional emulation.[1][2]
simulation
Computing the behaviour of an assumed model. Executability and correspondence to the original organism require separate tests.[3]
emulation
Here, reproducing an original system with functional correspondence over specified conditions and timescales. This differs from computationally reproducing a paper’s results.[4]
whole-brain emulation (WBE)
The research goal of emulating an original individual’s function across the whole brain. Whole-brain structural data or predictions for limited tasks alone do not establish its achievement.[3][4]
system identification
Inferring model form or parameters from observations and interventions. More than one candidate may fit the observations.[5][6]
inverse problem
Inferring states or models from observed outcomes. The observation process and assumptions must be specified.[5][7]
identifiability
Whether available observations and interventions can distinguish candidate models or parameters. It depends on the model class, measurement coverage and stimuli.[6]
degeneracy
Here, different circuit parameters producing similar activity. A fit to similar activity may therefore fail to determine a unique internal mechanism.[8]
fidelity
Correspondence between model and original for prespecified functions, conditions and timescales. More detail alone does not establish higher fidelity.[4]
closed loop
Interaction in which sensory input changes action and action changes subsequent sensory input. Body, environment and controller conditions must also be specified.[9]
sensory feedback
Information about the body or environment changed by action returning as sensory input.[9]
calcium imaging
Recording fluorescence related to intracellular calcium. The signal is not a direct record of membrane voltage or every spike; it passes through an observation process with temporal response and noise.[10]
relative change in fluorescence (ΔF/F)
The change in fluorescence relative to a reference fluorescence level. Specify the reference and preprocessing; this is not a direct measure of voltage or spike timing.[11]
engram
Physical changes or cell ensembles involved in memory retention and recall. Identifying candidates differs from recovering an individual’s memory in a model.[12]
dense-core vesicle
A secretory vesicle with an electron-dense core, involved in signalling such as neuropeptide release. It is a candidate source of information not fully captured by synaptic wiring.[13]
leaky integrate-and-fire model (LIF)
A simplified neuron model that integrates input with leak and emits a spike at a threshold. It does not describe every intracellular mechanism.[14]
optic flow estimation
Estimating a field of apparent image motion from changes over time. Used here for the training task of the Lappalainen et al. model.[15]
electron microscopy (EM)
Imaging fine structure using electrons. Report imaging scope and resolution separately from the connectivity inferred from the images.[16]
expansion microscopy (ExM)
Physically expanding a specimen to resolve finer detail by optical imaging. Expansion factor, labelling, deformation and readable structures require evaluation.[17][16]
expansion light-sheet fluorescence microscopy (ExLSFM)
Combining expansion microscopy with light-sheet fluorescence imaging. Collins et al.’s whole-brain acquisition estimates include untested combinations and assumptions about future performance.[16]
image segmentation
Partitioning an image into regions such as cells. Distinct from dividing data into training and evaluation sets.[1]
split and merge errors
Errors that split one cell into separate objects or merge different cells into one. These are image-reconstruction errors.[1]
data split
Separating data for training, tuning and evaluation. Splits must reflect time, session and individual dependence and the intended prediction target.[18]
held-out data
Here, data excluded from model training, tuning and selection. Independence must be checked against the split and prediction target.[18]
image reconstruction
In this guide’s workflow vocabulary, assembling cell morphology and connections from imaging data. Distinguished from model recovery or inference.[1]
model inference
Inferring model states, parameters or rules from observations. Whether original information was recovered correctly requires independent evaluation.[5]
proofreading
In connectome reconstruction, human checking and correction of errors such as tracing, splitting and merging errors from automated processing.[1]
calibration
Here, adjusting measurements or decoder outputs against reference data or a participant. Distinguished from manual correction of reconstruction errors.
computational reproduction
Here, checking reported computational results using the same data and code or analysis procedure. Distinct from emulating biological function.
independent replication
Here, checking a result with independently obtained data or an independent implementation. State exactly which data, implementation and investigators are independent.
completeness
How much of a specified set of cells, regions or connections a reconstruction includes. State the denominator and unknown coverage.[2]
synapse count
The number of counted synapses. Multiple synapses may join one cell pair, so this differs from the number of connected pairs or edges.[2][15]
simulation-based inference (SBI)
Using simulated data to infer parameters or other quantities compatible with observations. Approximate-posterior methods remain conditional on model, prior and observation assumptions.[19]
filter simulation-based inference (fSBI)
The method used by Confavreux et al. to filter distributions of candidate plasticity rules by applying activity and weight conditions sequentially. The resulting distribution need not be a true Bayesian posterior.[20]
approximate posterior
A probability distribution approximating uncertainty about parameters or other quantities after observation, conditional on a model and prior. Concentration does not guarantee a correct model.[19]
coverage probability
The probability that an interval contains the true value over repeated sampling and interval construction under specified generating conditions. Distinct from a posterior probability for one observed interval.[21]
equivalence margin
A boundary on differences considered equivalent. Denoted ε here and proposed to be set before inspecting results, using task meaning and independent measurements.[22]
error tolerance
The acceptable range of error for a purpose, such as acquisition or reconstruction. Specify the quantity and workflow step; distinguish it from a margin used to test equivalence.
two one-sided tests (TOST)
An equivalence-testing procedure that tests the lower and upper equivalence bounds separately. A nonsignificant conventional difference test alone does not establish equivalence.[22]
exchangeability
A condition under which the joint distribution is unchanged by specified label permutations under the null. It does not permit ignoring dependence or differences in sex or stage.
permutation test
A test that forms a comparison distribution by label permutations allowed under the null. Specify what is permuted and which dependencies are preserved.
Holm correction
A step-down procedure that compares ordered p-values with successively less stringent thresholds to control family-wise error across a specified family of tests.
statistical power
The probability that a planned test rejects its null under a specified true effect and data-generating conditions. It depends on effect size, variability, test and correction as well as sample size.[22]
noise ceiling
An estimated ceiling for a specified prediction metric based on repeat-measurement variability. It depends on measurements and estimation method and does not certify the mechanism.[23]
maximum attainable correlation coefficient (CCmax)
The correlation ceiling estimated from between-trial variability by the Schoppe et al. method, for specified responses and repeated-measurement conditions.[23]
normalized correlation coefficient (CCnorm)
In the Schoppe et al. method, a prediction correlation normalized by its estimated ceiling. Ceiling-estimation error and assumptions also require checking.[23]
mean absolute error (MAE)
The mean absolute difference between predictions and measurements over evaluated elements. Compare it using matched quantities, units and aggregation rules.[11]
squared correlation with the expected response (r²_ER, r^2_ER)
The squared correlation between model predictions and the expected response without trial-to-trial variation. Distinguish this target from its estimator and check assumptions of corrected estimates and intervals.[21]
estimate-centered credible interval (ECCI)
The ECCI interval procedure proposed by Pospisil and Bair. It depends on the paper’s response model, prior and other conditions and is not directly applicable to arbitrary neural data.[21]
signal-to-noise ratio (SNR)
A ratio of signal to noise magnitude. Specify what counts as signal and noise and whether the calculation uses variance, amplitude or another quantity.[21]
identification matrix
In this guide’s proposed comparison, a matrix whose rows are model-source individuals and columns are evaluated individuals, with prediction scores for each pair. Cell matching and model selection are fixed independently of test data.
timescale
The duration over which the evaluated phenomenon changes. Distinguish immediate responses, learning and long-term retention, and report measurement resolution separately.[4]
milestone
An intermediate research goal with assessable outputs and criteria. In this guide it is not a forecast of guaranteed achievement in a scheduled year.
review window
An editorial time to reassess progress and dependencies, not a predicted completion date.
gate criteria
Prespecified comparisons and outputs used to decide whether to advance to another research stage, judged against each experiment’s conditions and evidence.
aldehyde-stabilized cryopreservation (ASC)
A method combining aldehyde fixation and cryopreservation. Reported ultrastructural preservation is a separate claim from preservation or recovery of memory and function.[24]
Brain Modeling ToolKit (BMTK)
A software suite for building and simulating brain-circuit models at multiple levels of detail. Tool availability alone does not validate a resulting model.[25]
Open Science Framework (OSF)
A platform used for activities such as preregistration and sharing research materials. Registration alone does not validate a plan.
preprint
A manuscript made public before formal publication following peer review. Check the version read, release date and review/publication status.

Internal states, learning rules and observation models

The following is this site's conceptual notation, not an equation reproduced from a particular paper or a completed brain model.

x(t+Δt) = F(x(t), u(t); θ(t))
θ(t+Δt) = G_ψ(θ(t), x(t), u(t))
y_k = H_φ(x_{≤t_k}) + η_k

x denotes fast states such as voltage and adaptation; u, inputs including stimuli and reward; and θ, connection strengths and transmission properties. F describes responses; G, learning and plasticity; and ψ, parameters of the learning rule. State–parameter distinctions depend on timescale. Model form, such as point neurons versus multicompartment cells, also requires validation. Quantitative membrane-current models and input–output system identification provide foundations.[26][5]

y is the measured signal; H, an observation model mapping internal states to measured and preprocessed signals; φ, its calibration parameters; and η, observation error. Dependence on past states can represent calcium-response delays and temporal smoothing. Measured relative change in fluorescence (ΔF/F) and model voltage are not directly comparable quantities. A predictor that outputs ΔF/F directly can be evaluated in that observed signal space.[11]

Receptors and neuromodulation can change responses beyond what a connectome specifies. Test necessary detail by restoring omitted mechanisms and checking improvement on independent evaluation conditions.[13][4] The validation procedure specifies signal comparisons; learning-rule identification explains how to select G from data.

Information and limitations of each measurement method

MeasurementMain informationWhat it does not supply alone
Electron microscopy (EM)Fine morphology and synaptic connectivityFixed images do not directly reveal the previous activity history.
Expansion microscopy (ExM) and molecular labelsMorphology and locations of labelled moleculesLabel loss, distortion, registration and imaging volume require evaluation.
Electrophysiology and calcium imagingTemporal changes, stimulus responses and population activityNot every state of every cell is necessarily observed simultaneously.
Perturbation and behaviourIntervention responses and input–output relationsDifferent mechanisms may produce similar behaviour.
Glial labels, activity and selective interventionsCell types, learning-associated ensembles and modulation of neuronal activityMorphology alone does not specify functional state or memory content. Required states must be tested for each task.
Peptides, receptors and evoked responsesEffective signal propagation including extrasynaptic transmissionGene expression does not directly measure release or receptor state; evoked responses also include indirect paths.

MICrONS provides paired structure and activity in the same animal. LICONN combines roughly sixteenfold tissue expansion, dense optical reconstruction and molecular labels. Its largest imaged native tissue volume was approximately one million µm³ (0.001 mm³), rather than a whole brain. Some antibodies and conditions do not survive the workflow well, so molecular targets require individual validation.[10][17]

Compare resolution, volume, speed, missing data, cost and reconstruction errors together. Imaging-time estimates are not end-to-end estimates for preservation, reconstruction and functional validation.[16]

Transmission beyond wiring: Randi et al. (2023) optogenetically stimulated individual neurons in immobilized C. elegans. They examined signal propagation across 23,433 head-neuron pairs. Comparisons including unc-31 mutants affecting dense-core-vesicle release supported contributions from extrasynaptic signalling. The measured response includes direct and indirect paths; it is not a count of monosynaptic connections. Mismatch with a particular anatomical model does not establish failure of every wiring-based model.[27]

This does not establish that every glial or molecular state must be measured. Compare models with and without candidate mechanisms on the same held-out conditions to select useful measurements. See the omission tests.[28]

The inverse problem from data to model

Circuits with different parameter settings can produce similar activity. This degeneracy means that fitting observations need not yield a unique answer.[8]

Han and colleagues tested artificial vision models with known ground truth and found conditions in which activity-prediction scores did not select the correct model architecture. Identifiability also changed with unit coverage and stimulus choice. The lesson is to design observations that discriminate candidates, rather than to conclude that identification is impossible.[6]

As a research proposal, a set of models functionally equivalent over specified interventions can be a provisional target. State the tested conditions and do not assume equivalence outside them. Combine wiring, activity, task constraints and interventions to narrow the candidates.[15][6]

Estimate uncertainty: The simulation-based inference (SBI) approach used here estimates a distribution of parameters compatible with observations, rather than a single solution. This approximate posterior is written p(θ | y, M): θ denotes parameters, y observations and M the assumed model. The estimate also depends on the specified prior. Gonçalves and colleagues estimated multiple compatible solutions and compensatory parameter relationships in neural dynamics models. The distribution depends on the model, prior, observed features and noise assumptions. It does not by itself establish equivalence under arbitrary interventions or unique whole-brain reconstruction.[19]

Our proposed evaluation uses posterior predictions to distinguish candidates under held-out stimuli and interventions. It also checks interval coverage in artificial models with known ground truth and sensitivity to the prior. See M1 for the evaluation requirements.

What theory can establish: NETT analyses existence, stability and convergence for inverse problems with learned regularization under assumptions including continuity and coercivity. Its numerical examples reconstruct photoacoustic images. Here it serves as general inverse-problem theory, rather than a direct result about identifying neural circuits. Such guarantees help design constrained inference, but do not automatically guarantee unique recovery of a brain's true state.[7]

Specify the observation model H and calibrate φ or assess its uncertainty. Separate errors in circuit state or connectivity from measurement delay, mixing and noise. With learning trajectories, also distinguish initial strengths θ from the update rule G. Continue to learning-rule identification.

Memory-related structure and tests of recovery

Uytiepo et al. (2025) labelled cells active during fear learning in mice and examined local hippocampal synaptic structure one week later. The main EM comparison used three conditioned and two control animals, finding differences including terminals contacting multiple spines. The result was not simply preferential wiring among co-labelled cells. This detects learning-associated structural differences; it does not decode arbitrary memory content.[12]

A researcher survey also found varied views on the structural scale and information needed for memory recovery. Its broad definition of static structure includes individual biomolecules and assumes successful, ideal preservation. Response percentages are neither measured recovery probabilities nor proportions of memories preserved.[29]

The next experiments should link learning, measurement and held-out recall in the same individual, examining retention, forgetting, relearning and intervention responses. Keep test answers out of model fitting, so the experiment can assess whether retained information, rather than additional answer training, supports recall.

Next, link learning and measurement in the same individual. Reserve recall tasks not used for model adjustment. Use them to examine retention and forgetting, relearning and changes after interventions. Keep evaluation answers out of additional model training so that recovery of prior memory remains testable.

Preservation results and requirements for functional recovery

Aldehyde-stabilized cryopreservation (ASC) preserves the ultrastructure of chemically fixed tissue. The 2015 study examined, among other preparations, eight rabbits under the final protocol and one successfully processed pig. Images showed traceable neurites and synapses; restoration of physiological function or learned memories was not evaluated.[24]

The next experimental task is to measure what preservation changes and how those losses affect functional prediction. Retaining ultrastructure, retaining necessary states, reading out information and validating function are separate milestones.[30]

Learning-rule identification and tests on unseen conditions

A model that keeps learning needs G, the rule for changing its weights. Learning-rule inference fits candidate rules to neural or behavioural trajectories during learning. Keep initial strengths θ, rule parameters ψ and observation calibration φ distinct.

  • Recovery in artificial circuits: Mehta et al. recovered known rules in models with matched circuit architecture and tested noise and incomplete recordings. Their biological application evaluated candidates including weight decay against choices from 18 flies. Some flies had poor predictions for later observations; this does not identify a unique biological G.[31]
  • Retain alternative rules: Confavreux et al. searched families satisfying activity and weight constraints in spiking models. Different rules can produce similar activity, and filter simulation-based inference (fSBI) does not necessarily return a true Bayesian posterior.[20]
  • Constrain rules with biological interventions: Hige et al. found that depression induced by pairing odour and dopamine-neuron activation depended on stimulus order and mushroom-body compartment. These differences can test assumptions of a shared rule.[32]

Our proposed experiment:

  1. Pass artificial circuits with known G through observation model H, varying noise, missingness and structural errors. Score observed-signal prediction, weight trajectories and rule recovery separately.
  2. Use structural and intervention evidence from a circuit such as the mushroom body to constrain candidates. Fit multiple G models to learning trajectories, recording assumptions about architecture, initial weights, observation mapping and rule families.
  3. Reserve changed reward conditions, extinction, reversal learning and retention intervals for evaluation. Freeze G's parameters during evaluation while allowing G to update the weights. Do not refit G using evaluation answers.
  4. If predictions reproduce under unseen conditions, compare retention, forgetting and relearning over longer periods in the same individual, then enter route 3 with a body. If candidates remain indistinguishable, add measurements or interventions where predictions diverge. If all fail, revisit architecture, initial weights, observation models and rule families.

This is a research proposal. Known-rule recovery in artificial circuits, fitting limited fly tasks, long-term whole-brain learning and recovery of previously acquired memories each require separate validation.
